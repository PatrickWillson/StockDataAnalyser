# Graham Methodology Reference

> **Purpose:** the reference agents use to decide *what Graham would do* before changing any formula, threshold, investor test, verdict rule or data source in this codebase.
> **How to use it:** find the relevant section, apply its rule, and cite the section (e.g. "GRAHAM_METHODOLOGY §4.6") in the change description. If the code and this document disagree, the code is wrong unless a decision recorded here says otherwise (§9).
> Compiled 2026-10-25. Graham's books are under copyright and were not available in full text; this document therefore relies on Graham's own published lectures and interviews where possible and labels every claim by source quality.

- **[Q]** A verbatim quotation of *The Intelligent Investor* (4th ed., 1973) or *Security Analysis* reproduced by a secondary source.

## 1. Core philosophy

1. **Investment vs speculation.** An investment operation promises safety of principal and an adequate return after thorough analysis; anything else is speculation. **[S]**
2. **Margin of safety is the central concept** (*Intelligent Investor* ch. 20). For a common stock bought at a sensible price, the margin of safety is the excess of its earnings yield (earnings ÷ price) over the bond interest rate: *"Over a ten year period, the typical excess of stock earning power over bond interest may aggregate 50% of the price paid. This is sufficient to provide a very real margin of safety."* **[Q]** For bargain issues the margin is the discount of price below appraised value. **[S]**
3. **Simple, conservative criteria beat forecasts.** In 1976 Graham said he had lost interest in elaborate security analysis: *"I think we can do it successfully with a few techniques and simple principles. The main point is to have the right general principles and the character to stick to them."* He favoured applying *"some simple criterion for being undervalued"* to groups of stocks, and noted *"people do not succeed in forecasting what's going to happen to the stock market."* **[P]**
4. **Facts from the record, not projections.** Graham's valuations rest on past audited results averaged over a stated period; growth projections are treated as unreliable (see §6). **[P]/[S]**

## 2. Earning power (earnings measures)

1. **Definition.** *"Past earning power … should mean the average earnings over a stated period which would ordinarily be identified in the discussion. But if not so identified it would be some representative period such as five or seven or perhaps ten years in the past."* Future earning power is ordinarily *"the average earnings over the next five years."* **[P]** (Lecture 4)
2. **Security Analysis:** look at profits for *"not less than five years, preferably seven or ten years."* (1934, p. 452) **[Q]**
3. **Never one year alone; special items stay in.** *The Intelligent Investor* ch. 12: analysts used *"the average earnings over a fairly long period in the past—usually from seven to ten years"*, and averaging *"will solve the problem of what to do about nearly all the special charges and credits. They should be included in the average earnings. For certainly most of these losses and gains represent a part of the company's operating history."* (ALCOA: 1961–70 average $3.62, 1964–70 $4.62.) **[Q]** So earnings means net income **for the common stock** (after minority interest and preferred dividends) **including** special and non-recurring items — never a "continuing operations" figure.
4. **Per-share basis, adjusted for capital changes.** Graham works in per-share figures and adjusts for capital transactions: *"That gain should be adjusted for items that do not relate to earnings … The transfer to a per share basis can be made at any convenient time."* **[P]** (Lecture 2) His per-share tables in *The Intelligent Investor* are footnoted *"adjusted for subsequent stock splits"* and *"adjusted for stock dividends and stock splits"* **[Q]** (snippets of an unofficial copy of the book; wording not verified against a licensed edition): a per-share history is restated on **one share basis** (today's), so earnings, dividends, book value and prices in different years are comparable.
5. **Balance-sheet check on reported earnings.** *"Subtract the balance sheet value shown at the beginning from that at the end of the period, and add back the dividends. This sum — adjusted for capital transactions — will give you the earnings that were actually realized by the company over the period."* He used it to expose earnings hidden or overstated by reserves. **[P]** (Lectures 1 and 2)
6. **Capitalising earning power.** Value = earning power × a multiplier *"which has to be kept within a reasonable range of variation."* Multipliers of 4 versus 40 for liked/disliked companies are *"not … admissible security-analysis technique."* **[P]** (Lecture 4)
7. **Buying multiple (1976).** *"I want to double the interest rate in terms of earnings return … A maximum multiple of 10 even when interest rates are under five percent, and a maximum multiple of 7 times even when interest rates are above seven percent."* I.e. buy when the earnings yield ≥ 2 × AAA bond yield, with P/E capped between 7 and 10. **[P]**

**Rules for this project [I]:**
- Multi-year earnings measures average **reported fiscal years only** (never placeholder or dividend-only rows), and the dossier **states the period used**.
- If fewer years exist than the method needs, show **N/A with the reason** — never 0 and never an average over a shorter period than the method allows.
- Average **per-share** earnings (each year's earnings ÷ that year's shares), not total earnings ÷ today's share count.
- Use annual audited statements; TTM is supplementary only and never feeds Graham tests.

## 3. Asset values

1. **Tangible equity** is what a buyer gets for his money: in Lecture 1 Graham values a prospectus by comparing price with *"$1,350,000 worth of tangible equity. Hence he was paying three times the amount of money invested in the business."* **[P]**
2. **Working capital and net current assets.** Graham repeatedly cites companies selling below working capital (*"Curtiss-Wright selling at two thirds or less of its working capital alone"*). **[P]**
3. **Net-net (bargain) issues.** Buying at two-thirds of net current asset value, as a diversified group: *"if a stock with $50 working capital sells at $32, that would be an interesting stock. If you buy 30 companies of that sort, you're bound to make money … if you buy stocks at two-thirds of the working capital value, you have a dependable indication of group undervaluation."* **[P]** (1976) Net current asset value is *"current assets alone, minus all liabilities and claims ahead of the issue"* (*Security Analysis*, 1934, as quoted by AAII), with preferred stock treated as such a claim (*"an imperfect creditorship position"*). **[Q]** *Security Analysis* ch. 43 (1940) adds that *"all true liabilities shown on the books must be deducted at their face amount"* and gives liquidation haircuts (cash 100%, receivables 75–90%, inventories 50–75%, fixed assets 1–50%) for a stricter liquidating value. **[Q]** Minority interests are among those claims: a *Security Analysis* worked example adjusts working capital *"to exclude the interest of the minority stockholders of a subsidiary"* and counts *"net current assets available for the stock, including interest in such assets of subsidiaries"*. **[S]** (7th-edition excerpt of the original text) The code deducts the whole minority interest (not only its share of subsidiary current assets): the conservative reading (§8.4).
4. **Book value in the defensive test** is *"the book value last reported."* **[Q]** Asset tests elsewhere (enterprising price test, net-nets) use **net tangible assets**. **[S]**
5. **Book value means tangible book value, after senior claims.** *Security Analysis* ch. 42 (1940 edition): *"The book value of a stock is the value of the assets applicable thereto as shown in the balance sheet. It is customary to restrict this value to the tangible assets"*, eliminating *"such items as good-will, trade names, patents, franchises, leaseholds."* *"The book value per share of a common stock is found by adding up all the tangible assets, subtracting all liabilities and stock issues ahead of the common and then dividing by the number of shares."* Preferred stock is subtracted *"at its proper valuation"* — ordinarily its par or stated value, or *"par (plus back dividends) or market, whichever is higher"*. **[Q]** (transcription at github.com/gusaiani/security-analysis-graham-dodd, chapter 42). So: **TBVPS = (common equity − goodwill − intangibles − preferred stock) ÷ shares**, used for the Graham Number and the defensive P/B test. (Decision 2026-10-31 for tangible book is therefore Graham's own definition, not an interpretation.)
6. **Leases count as debt.** *Security Analysis* treats annual rentals on leased property as fixed charges *"equivalent to bond interest"*, to be included where leases are material (e.g. retailers). **[S]** (7th-edition excerpt and chapter summaries) Hence total debt includes lease obligations, and on the free path so does long-term debt in the debt tests (§8.10, §9).
7. **A net-net needs no earnings history, but a bargain must not be consuming its assets.** The test is on assets alone, and Graham reported using it as such: *"the purchase of common stocks at less than their working-capital value, or net-current-asset value, giving no weight to the plant and other fixed assets"* with *"an average of some 20 per cent per year"*, and *"if you buy 30 companies of that sort, you're bound to make money"*. **[P]** (1976) Yet he also discriminated. *Security Analysis* ch. 43: these stocks *"on the whole"* are undervalued, but *"the analyst will avoid issues that have been losing their current assets at a rapid rate and show no definite signs of ceasing to do so"*; *"investment bargains"* are those that *"(1) are selling below their liquid-asset value, (2) are apparently in no danger of dissipating these assets, and (3) have formerly shown a large earning power on the market price."* **[Q]** *The Intelligent Investor*: two-thirds of working capital *"and if the earnings record and prospects are reasonably satisfactory"*. **[Q]** (as quoted by AAII) Summaries say the 1973 edition screens out companies with net losses in the last 12 months **[S]** (wording not verified against the book). **Decision (2026-11-06, user: decide what Graham would do):** a dossier judges one company, not a group, so price ≤ ⅔ NCAV is a NET-NET BARGAIN only if the latest year shows a profit and the discount outlasts any fall in NCAV per share for at least two years: (NCAV per share − price) ÷ last year's fall ≥ 2. Otherwise UNDERVALUED – FAILS GRAHAM CRITERIA, with the reason given; no earlier balance sheet also fails. Earnings history is not required. Graham gives no number for "rapid": two years is his holding period, *"until he had a 50% gain on it or until he had held it for two years"* **[S]** (AAII), and he compares balance sheets over time to judge this (Hupp Motors against Manhattan Shirt, ch. 43) **[Q]**. Per share, because a buyback is no loss to the holder (Keros 2025: current assets 89M to 15M after a 94.4M tender offer). **[I]**

## 4. Defensive investor — the seven criteria (*Intelligent Investor* ch. 14)

| # | Criterion | Graham's rule | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Adequate size | Industrials ≥ $100m annual sales; utilities ≥ $50m total assets (1972 dollars) | [S] |
| 4.2 | Strong financial condition | Industrials: current assets ≥ 2 × current liabilities **and** long-term debt ≤ net current assets (working capital = CA − CL). Public utilities: debt ≤ 2 × stock equity at book value; the current-ratio test is dropped | [S] (several summaries agree) |
| 4.3 | Earnings stability | Some earnings (no deficit) in each of the past 10 years | [S] |
| 4.4 | Dividend record | Uninterrupted payments for at least the past 20 years | [S] |
| 4.5 | Earnings growth | Per-share earnings up at least one-third over 10 years, **using three-year averages at the beginning and end** | [S] — window **decided 2026-11-01** (user, from the one published reading found, GrahamValue): latest 3 reported years vs the reported years 9–11 back |
| 4.6 | Moderate P/E | *Current price not more than 15 × average earnings of the past three years* | [S] (consistent across sources) |
| 4.7 | Moderate price-to-assets | *"Current price should not be more than 1½ times the book value last reported."* But *"a multiplier of earnings below 15 could justify a correspondingly higher multiplier of assets"*: *"the product of the multiplier times the ratio of price to book value should not exceed 22.5."* | [Q] |

Graham frames these as a *"minimum of quality"* plus a *"minimum of quantity"* (earnings and assets per dollar of price), providing a *"factor of safety"*. **[Q]**

**The "Graham Number"** √(22.5 × EPS × BVPS) is not a formula Graham published; it is derived from 4.6 × 4.7 (15 × 1.5 = 22.5) and is the highest price passing both. Faithful to Graham, EPS is the **three-year average**. **[S]**

## 5. Enterprising investor (*Intelligent Investor* ch. 15)

| # | Criterion | Graham's rule | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Primary filter | Low multiple: P/E below 9–10 on trailing earnings; for cyclical companies use **average earnings of the last three years** | [S] (AAII) |
| 5.2 | Financial condition | Current ratio ≥ 1.5; long-term debt ≤ 110% of net current assets | [S] |
| 5.3 | Earnings stability | No deficit in the last five years | [S] |
| 5.4 | Dividend record | Some current dividend | [S] |
| 5.5 | Earnings growth | Last year's earnings higher than those of about five years earlier (1966 in the 1972 text) | [S] |
| 5.6 | Price | Less than 120% of net tangible assets | [S] |

All criteria apply **together** (AND); the low-P/E filter is a starting screen, not an alternative to the asset test. No minimum size for the enterprising investor. **[S]** AAII's account of ch. 15 (Oct 2012) matches 5.1–5.6: P/E *"below nine or 10 times trailing earnings"* (three-year average earnings for cyclicals), current ratio ≥ 1.5, long-term debt ≤ 110% of working capital, positive earnings in each of the last five years, some current dividend, latest earnings above those of five years earlier, price-to-book ≤ 1.2 (net tangible assets). **[S]**

## 6. Growth-stock formula (use with care)

V = EPS × (8.5 + 2g) (1962/1973), revised 1974 to V = EPS × (8.5 + 2g) × 4.4 / Y, where g = expected 7–10 year growth and Y = current AAA yield. Graham warned the material was *"for illustrative purposes only"*, that projections are unreliable, and to *"limit your appraisals to enterprises of investment quality."* **[Q]** **Project rule [I]:** do not use it for verdicts; it depends on a forecast.

## 7. Margin of safety and the verdict (project design, D8)

Graham's principles applied to a verdict **[I]**:
- A purchase needs **both** a qualifying company (passing the investor's criteria) **and** a margin of safety; "N/A" is never evidence of quality.
- Net-current-asset bargains (price ≤ ⅔ NCAV per share) are their own category (§3.3).
- A price above conservative value is never a buy, whatever other tests say.
- Thresholds such as "≥ 20% margin = strong buy" are not Graham's and should not drive verdicts.

The ordered rules, implemented in `core/verdict.py` (F20): latest annual report over 18 months old → INSUFFICIENT DATA (§8.8); price ≤ ⅔ × NCAV per share → NET-NET BARGAIN if the latest year shows a profit and the discount covers at least two years of the fall in NCAV per share, otherwise UNDERVALUED – FAILS GRAHAM CRITERIA (§3.7, decided 2026-11-06); no intrinsic value → INSUFFICIENT DATA; margin of safety ≤ 0 or N/A → OVERVALUED; all defensive criteria pass → DEFENSIVE BUY; all enterprising criteria pass → ENTERPRISING BUY; otherwise UNDERVALUED – FAILS GRAHAM CRITERIA. **[I]** (rule order is this project's design; each rule applies a principle above)

**This codebase's EPV** = average net income (7–10 years) ÷ 10% is a simplified *capitalised past earning power* (§2.1, §2.6): a multiplier of 10, within Graham's range. "EPV" as a name comes from Greenwald (2001), whose method differs (normalised EBIT, maintenance capex, WACC). **[I]**

## 8. Data rules derived from Graham (for this project) [I]

1. Use **raw annual audited statement data**; derive every ratio here (CLAUDE.md "Raw data in, our own calculations out"; guide §5.0).
2. Multi-year figures: reported fiscal years only, stated period, N/A below the method's minimum (§2).
3. Per-share figures use each year's own share count; adjust for splits and capital transactions (§2.4).
4. Prefer conservative choices when data is ambiguous (e.g. exclude, don't impute).
5. TTM data is informational; Graham tests use annual data only.
6. Document every assumption and conversion in the dossier.
7. **Restated years: use the latest restated figure, and flag it** (user decision 2026-11-01, for sources that carry every filing's version of a year, e.g. SEC EDGAR). Graham judges each year by what it really earned on a consistent basis, using later knowledge, not by the figure first reported: he compares balance sheets "to obtain a check on the reported earnings" and to determine "what we might call true earnings, as compared with reported earnings", raising one company's earlier earnings because its reserves "did not constitute in any sense real liabilities, because in July 1946…" and lowering another's because its reserves "proved insufficient" **[P]** (Lecture 2); he restates railway figures for tax carry-backs and rental payments to make them comparable **[P]** (Lecture 3). A later filing's restatement (error correction, retrospective accounting change) is the company's own correction on that basis; Graham does not discuss restated filings as such, so applying his practice to them is **[I]**. Data Quality lists every year and field whose value differs from the first-reported one.
8. **No verdict once a later annual report must exist** (decided 2026-11-06, user: go with what Graham would do). Graham names *"the inadequacy or incorrectness of the data"* first among the analyst's obstacles; where information is lacking he should *"note this defect and make allowance therefor"* (*Security Analysis* ch. 1) **[Q]**. *"Every listed company publishes an annual report"* (ch. 3) **[Q]**, and his test uses *"the book value last reported"* **[Q]**. Past 18 months from the period end (12 to the next year end, up to 4 to file, a margin), the figures we hold are not those last reported: the verdict is INSUFFICIENT DATA, with the figures still shown and the staleness warning kept. The 18 months is ours; Graham gives no number. **[I]** (MicroAlgo: moved from 10-K to 20-F, a 2022 balance sheet against a 2026 price.)
9. **Long-term debt not identified is unknown, not zero** (decided 2026-11-08, user: do what Graham would do). Graham counts all funded debt and warns that a reported "no funded debt" can hide fixed obligations (United Cigar Stores, 1928: *"reported 'no funded debt' … Yet so crushing were the liabilities under its long-term leases … that in 1932 bankruptcy was resorted to"*, *Security Analysis* ch. 17) **[Q]**; where information is lacking the analyst should *"note this defect and make allowance therefor"* (ch. 1) **[Q]**. Equally, the absence of debt is genuine and desirable (*"many of them have no bonds outstanding"*; *"the absence of funded debt is a desirable feature"*) **[Q]**. So: no borrowing item in the filing → long-term debt is zero and the debt tests can pass; borrowing items reported but no long-term debt figure identified (free path: `BORROWINGS_EVIDENCE` in clients/edgar_mapper.py) → long-term debt is unknown, the debt tests (4.2, 5.2) are not met, and the dossier says "not identified" instead of "Debt-free". **[I]** (Cellectis, Inventiva, Nanobiotix; before their debt tags were mapped, Novartis and Unilever showed "Debt-free (excellent)".)
10. **Lease liabilities count as debt** (decided 2026-11-08, user: do what Graham would do). Graham treats rentals on leased property as fixed charges *"equivalent to bond interest"* where the rental factor is important, and his example of a company that reported "no funded debt" and went bankrupt under its leases is a retailer (*Security Analysis* ch. 17) **[Q]**. He left open whether rentals count "in whole or in part" **[Q]**; balance sheets now carry lease obligations as liabilities (IFRS 16, ASC 842), so the conservative reading (§8.4) counts them whole: free path, non-current lease liabilities are in long-term debt (the debt tests 4.2, 5.2) and current ones in short-term debt (operating and finance leases; finance leases already inside a "…AndCapitalLeaseObligations" debt tag are not added again). **[I]** Effect on 105 cached US filers: the defensive debt test flips for 5 (Bassett, Caleres, Genesco, Carter's, Microsoft). Pre-2019 US years have no operating-lease liability on the balance sheet, so those years count finance leases only.
11. **Vendor figures only behind the filings, field by field, never rescaled** (decided 2026-11-08 for the European route; user: think about what Graham would do). Graham's analyst drew on the statistical manuals of his day (Poor's, Moody's) as well as companies' own reports (*Security Analysis* ch. 3, per a secondary summary) **[S]**, and where data is inadequate should *"note this defect and make allowance therefor"* (ch. 1) **[Q]**. So vendor history (Eulerpool) is used only for years before the filed (ESEF) history, labelled as vendor data, and only for fields that agreed with the filed figure in every overlapping year (at least two; within 1%, 0.5% when the reporting currency is not the listing's, where the vendor's errors clustered). A field that disagreed is not used for the older years: inferring a conversion or scale ratio from the overlap would be an estimate, not the reported figure (§8.4). Years labelled a year apart from the filings, or figures in another currency, rule out all of the vendor's years. Net income and the year-end share count must both pass the gate, but the vendor years carry no per-share figures at all (§8.12); a year whose vendor count jumps and reverts (Games Workshop FY2007-09: 62M between years of 31M) is left out, not imputed (decided 2026-11-09). Companies with no filings at all on any route (Germany, Ireland, AIM) take Yahoo Finance's statements for their recent years and the vendor's older years behind the same gate against them (§8.12); only where Yahoo has no statements does the earlier vendor-only rule apply (user, 2026-11-08: include, labelled as unverified vendor data), checked against Yahoo Finance: same reporting currency, net income within 2% (or 1.1M, whole millions) in every overlapping year (at least two), latest share count within 10%; every figure is marked unverified. Their long-term debt is all non-current liabilities, an upper bound (decided 2026-11-09, §8.4 conservative figure): Eulerpool's own long-term debt includes lease liabilities for some companies (Hunting, Clas Ohlson) and not others (Tesco, Renishaw, Manitou, Brunel, Norcros), and is 0 for some with debt (Games Workshop), so it cannot show the debt Graham counts (§8.10); the upper bound can fail a company whose debt alone would pass, never pass one that should fail; without the totals, unknown (§8.9).
12. **Statistical services where there is no filed XBRL; no per-share figures from vendor history** (decided 2026-11-09; user: deep dive into what Graham would do, else the recommended options; claude-output/YAHOO_STATEMENTS_SCOPE.md). Graham: *"Most of the information required by the securities analyst in his daily work may be found conveniently and adequately presented by the various statistical services ... These services have made great progress during the past 20 years in the completeness and accuracy with which they present the facts. Nevertheless they cannot be relied upon to give all the data available in the various original sources ... the analyst should consult the original reports and other documents wherever possible, and not rely upon summaries or transcriptions"*, and *"the chief source of statistical data is, of course, the reports issued to the stockholders"* (*Security Analysis* ch. 3, 1940 text) **[Q]**. So: (a) the company's own filed XBRL (SEC, ESEF) stays the source wherever it exists; (b) for companies with none on the free routes, Yahoo Finance's annual statements (about four years; the more accurate of the two services tested, 94-100% exact against filed figures, against 17-91% for Eulerpool) are used for the recent years, labelled as a statistical service, and Eulerpool's older years must agree with them field by field (§8.11); (c) long-term debt in Yahoo years is its debt-and-lease line where a lease line is reported, else all non-current liabilities, an upper bound (§8.4, §8.10); (d) **vendor (Eulerpool) years keep net income, revenue, balance totals and cash flow but no share count, EPS or other per-share figure**: §2.4 and §8.3 need each year's own share count, and the vendor's long-run counts cannot be checked (spikes, unstated restatements); excluded, not imputed with today's count (§8.4). A test that needs a per-share figure from such a year is N/A, never a pass (§7): defensive 4.5 and enterprising 5.5 earnings growth, and EPV per share when its 7-10 year window reaches those years (the whole-company EPV still averages net income). **[I]** for (b)-(d): Graham names no service or rule for vendor share counts.
